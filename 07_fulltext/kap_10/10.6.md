# Section 10.6: Cylindrical and Spherical Coordinates

[sid 603]

Polar coordinates provide a useful alternative to plane Cartesian coordinates for describing plane regions with circular symmetry or bounded by arcs of circles centred at the origin and radial lines from the origin. Similarly, there are two commonly encountered alternatives to Cartesian coordinates in 3-space. They generalize plane polar coordinates to 3-space and are suitable for describing regions with cylindrical or spherical symmetry. We introduce these two coordinate systems here, but won't make much use of them until the latter part of Chapter 14 when we will learn how to integrate over such regions.

## Cylindrical Coordinates

Among the most useful alternatives to Cartesian coordinates in 3-space is the coordinate system that directly generalizes plane polar coordinates by replacing only the horizontal x and y coordinates with the polar coordinates r and θ, while leaving the vertical z coordinate untouched. This system is called cylindrical coordinates. Each point in 3-space has cylindrical coordinates [r, θ, z] related to its Cartesian coordinates (x, y, z) by the transformation

    x = r cos θ,    y = r sin θ,    z = z.

The distance from a point P to the z-axis is r, while the distance from P to the origin is

    d = sqrt(r^2 + z^2) = sqrt(x^2 + y^2 + z^2).

[FIGUR 10.38: A point P showing both Cartesian coordinates (x, y, z) and cylindrical coordinates [r, θ, z]]

Just as planes with equations x = x_0, y = y_0, and z = z_0 are the coordinate surfaces containing the point (x_0, y_0, z_0) of the Cartesian coordinate system in 3-space, so also the coordinate surfaces containing the point [r_0, θ_0, z_0] in cylindrical coordinates are:
    the r-surface with equation r = r_0 (the vertical circular cylinder centred on the z-axis),
    the θ-surface θ = θ_0 (the vertical half-plane with edge along the z-axis),
    and
    the z-surface z = z_0 (the horizontal plane).

[FIGUR 10.39: The coordinate surfaces for cylindrical coordinates]

Cylindrical coordinates lend themselves to representing domains that are bounded by such surfaces and, in particular, to problems with axial symmetry (around the z-axis).

The coordinate curves in the cylindrical coordinate system are intersections of pairs of coordinate surfaces:
    The r-curves are the intersections of the planes θ = constant and z = constant, and so are horizontal radial lines emanating from the z-axis.
    The θ-curves are intersections of the cylinders r = constant and planes z = constant, and so are horizontal circles centred on the z-axis.
    The z-curves are intersections of the cylinders r = constant and the half-planes θ = constant, and so are vertical straight lines.

## EXAMPLE 1 - Converting cylindrical to Cartesian

The point with Cartesian coordinates (1, 1, 1) has cylindrical coordinates [sqrt(2), π/4, 1]. The point with Cartesian coordinates (0, 2, -3) has cylindrical coordinates [2, π/2, -3]. The point with cylindrical coordinates [4, -π/3, 5] has Cartesian coordinates (2, -2sqrt(3), 5).

## EXAMPLE 2 - Identifying surfaces from cylindrical equations

Identify the surfaces whose equations in cylindrical coordinates are:
(a) z = r^2,
(b) z = r cos θ,
(c) r = 2 cos θ.

### Solution

(a) z = r^2 represents the circular paraboloid with Cartesian equation z = x^2 + y^2. It has vertex at the origin and axis of symmetry along the positive z-axis.

(b) z = r cos θ represents the plane with Cartesian equation z = x. It contains the y-axis and the point with Cartesian coordinates (1, 0, 1).

(c) r = 2 cos θ can be rewritten r^2 = 2r cos θ, so represents the vertical surface with Cartesian equation x^2 + y^2 = 2x. This is a circular cylinder of radius 1 with central axis along the vertical line through the point (1, 0, 0) (in Cartesian coordinates).

## EXAMPLE 3 - Identifying curves from cylindrical equations

Describe the curves whose equations in cylindrical coordinates are:
(a) r = z, z = 1 + r cos θ,
(b) θ = π/2, r^2 + z^2 = 4.

### Solution

(a) The curve is the parabola in which the plane z = 1 + x intersects the right-circular half-cone z = sqrt(x^2 + y^2). Since the plane is parallel to the line z = x, which is a generator of the cone, the intersection must be a parabola rather than an ellipse or a hyperbola.

(b) θ = π/2 represents the half of the yz-plane where y >= 0. r^2 + z^2 = 4 represents a sphere of radius 2 centred at the origin. Thus, this curve is the semicircle with cartesian equation y = sqrt(4 - z^2) in the plane x = 0.

## Spherical Coordinates

[sid 605]

In the system of spherical coordinates a point P in 3-space is represented by the ordered triple [R, φ, θ], where R is the distance from P to the origin O, φ (Greek "phi") is the angle the radial line OP makes with the positive direction of the z-axis, and θ is the angle between the plane containing P and the z-axis and the xz-plane. It is conventional to consider spherical coordinates restricted in such a way that R >= 0, 0 <= φ <= π, and 0 <= θ < 2π (or -π < θ <= π). Every point not on the z-axis then has exactly one spherical coordinate representation, and the transformation from Cartesian coordinates (x, y, z) to spherical coordinates [R, φ, θ] is one-to-one off the z-axis. Using right-angled triangles, we can see that this transformation is given by:

    x = R sin φ cos θ,
    y = R sin φ sin θ,
    z = R cos φ.

Observe that

    R^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = r^2 + z^2

and that the r coordinate in cylindrical coordinates is related to R and φ by

    r = sqrt(x^2 + y^2) = R sin φ.

Thus, also

    tan φ = r/z = sqrt(x^2 + y^2) / z    and    tan θ = y/x.

If φ = 0 or φ = π, then r = 0, so the θ coordinate is irrelevant at points on the z-axis.

[FIGUR 10.40: The spherical coordinates of a point [R, φ, θ]]

The coordinate surfaces containing point [R_0, φ_0, θ_0] in spherical coordinates are shown in Figure 10.41. They are:
    the R-surface R = R_0, the sphere centred at the origin,
    the φ-surface φ = φ_0, the nappe of the circular cone with the z-axis as axis,
    and
    the θ-surface θ = θ_0, the vertical half-planes with edge along the z-axis.

[FIGUR 10.41: The coordinate surfaces for spherical coordinates]

Similarly, pairs of coordinate surfaces intersect in coordinate curves along which only one of the coordinates varies:
    The R-curves (along which only R varies) are the intersections of φ- and θ-surfaces, and so are radial lines emanating from the origin.
    The φ-curves (along which only φ varies) are the intersections of R- and θ-surfaces, and so are vertical semicircles centred at the origin and beginning and ending on the z-axis.
    The θ-curves (along which only θ varies) are the intersections of the R- and φ-surfaces, and thus are horizontal circles with centres on the z-axis.

If we take a coordinate system with origin at the centre of the earth, z-axis through the north pole, and x-axis through the intersection of the Greenwich meridian and the equator, then the earth's surface is (roughly speaking) an R-surface. Its intersections with the φ-surfaces are θ-curves on the earth's surface and are called parallels of latitude. The intersections of the surface of the earth with the θ-surfaces are φ-curves, called meridians of longitude. Since latitude is measured from 90° at the north pole to -90° at the south pole, while φ is measured from 0 at the north pole to π (= 180°) at the south pole, the coordinate φ is frequently referred to as the colatitude coordinate; θ is the longitude coordinate. Observe that θ has the same significance in spherical coordinates as it does in cylindrical coordinates.

Spherical coordinates are suited to problems involving spherical symmetry and, in particular, to regions bounded by spheres centred at the origin, circular cones with axes along the z-axis, and vertical planes containing the z-axis.

## EXAMPLE 4 - Converting between coordinate systems

Find:
(a) the Cartesian coordinates of the point P with spherical coordinates [2, π/3, π/2], and
(b) the spherical coordinates of the point Q with Cartesian coordinates (1, 1, sqrt(2)).

### Solution

(a) If R = 2, φ = π/3, and θ = π/2, then

    x = 2 sin(π/3) cos(π/2) = 0
    y = 2 sin(π/3) sin(π/2) = sqrt(3)
    z = 2 cos(π/3) = 1.

The Cartesian coordinates of P are (0, sqrt(3), 1).

(b) Given that

    R sin φ cos θ = x = 1
    R sin φ sin θ = y = 1
    R cos φ = z = sqrt(2),

we calculate that R^2 = 1^2 + 1^2 + (sqrt(2))^2 = 4, so R = 2. Also r = sqrt(1^2 + 1^2) = sqrt(2), so r = sqrt(2). Thus, tan φ = r/z = 1, so φ = π/4. Also, tan θ = y/x = 1, so θ = π/4 or 5π/4. Since x > 0, we must have θ = π/4. The spherical coordinates of Q are [2, π/4, π/4].

Remark: We write spherical coordinates in the order R, φ, θ rather than R, θ, φ. The reason concerns the triad of unit vectors at any point P, taken in coordinate order and tangent to the corresponding coordinate curve in the direction of increase of that coordinate. The order R, φ, θ ensures that this triad is a right-handed basis rather than a left-handed one.
