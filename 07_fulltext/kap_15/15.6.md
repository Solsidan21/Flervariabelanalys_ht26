# Section 15.6: Change of Variables in Triple Integrals
> Studieanteckningar baserade på Adams & Essex, Calculus 9th ed, sid 849-856
> Kursens numrering (Adams 10:e uppl.) = 15.6. Motsvarar 14.6 i 9:e uppl., som texten är hämtad ur.

The change of variables formula for a double integral extends to triple (and higher-order) integrals. Consider the transformation

    x = x(u, v, w),
    y = y(u, v, w),
    z = z(u, v, w),

where x, y, and z have continuous first partial derivatives with respect to u, v, and w. Near any point where the Jacobian partial(x,y,z)/partial(u,v,w) is nonzero, the transformation scales volume elements according to the formula

    dV = dx dy dz = | partial(x,y,z)/partial(u,v,w) | du dv dw.

Thus, if the transformation is one-to-one from a domain S in uvw-space onto a domain D in xyz-space, and if

    g(u, v, w) = f(x(u, v, w), y(u, v, w), z(u, v, w)),

then

    tripleintegral over D of f(x, y, z) dx dy dz = tripleintegral over S of g(u, v, w) | partial(x,y,z)/partial(u,v,w) | du dv dw.

The proof is similar to that of the two-dimensional case given in Section 14.4.

## EXAMPLE 1

Under the change of variables x = au, y = bv, z = cw, where a, b, c > 0, the solid ellipsoid E given by

    x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2 + z^2/c^2 <= 1

becomes the ball B given by u^2 + v^2 + w^2 <= 1. The Jacobian of this transformation is

    partial(x,y,z)/partial(u,v,w) = | a  0  0 |
                                     | 0  b  0 | = abc,
                                     | 0  0  c |

so the volume of the ellipsoid is given by

    Volume of E = tripleintegral over E of dx dy dz
                = tripleintegral over B of abc du dv dw = abc · (Volume of B)
                = (4/3) abc cubic units.

## Cylindrical Coordinates

In Section 10.6 we introduced the system of cylindrical coordinates r, theta, z in 3-space, related to Cartesian coordinates by the transformation

    x = r cos(theta),     y = r sin(theta),    z = z.

The geometric significance of these coordinates is shown in the figures related to cylindrical coordinate systems. The coordinate surfaces for cylindrical coordinates are cylinders of constant r, half-planes of constant theta, and planes of constant z.

As noted previously, cylindrical coordinates lend themselves to representing domains that are bounded by such surfaces and, in general, to problems with axial symmetry (around the z-axis).

The volume element in cylindrical coordinates is

    dV = r dr d(theta) dz,

which is easily seen by examining the infinitesimal "box" bounded by the coordinate surfaces corresponding to values r, r + dr, theta, theta + d(theta), z, and z + dz, or by calculating the Jacobian

    partial(x,y,z)/partial(r,theta,z) = | cos(theta)  -r sin(theta)  0 |
                                         | sin(theta)   r cos(theta)  0 | = r.
                                         | 0            0             1 |

## EXAMPLE 2

[sid 851]

Evaluate tripleintegral over D of (x^2 + y^2) dV over the first octant region bounded by the cylinders x^2 + y^2 = 1 and x^2 + y^2 = 4 and the planes z = 0, z = 1, x = 0, and x = y.

Solution In terms of cylindrical coordinates, the region is bounded by r = 1, r = 2, theta = pi/4, theta = pi/2, z = 0, and z = 1. It is a rectangular coordinate box in r-theta-z-space. Since the integrand is x^2 + y^2 = r^2, the integral is

    tripleintegral over D of (x^2 + y^2) dV = integral from 0 to 1 of dz integral from pi/4 to pi/2 of d(theta) integral from 1 to 2 of r^2 · r dr
                                             = (1 - 0) · (pi/2 - pi/4) · (2^4/4 - 1^4/4)
                                             = 1 · (pi/4) · (15/4)
                                             = 15pi/16.

This integral would have been much more difficult to evaluate using Cartesian coordinates.

## EXAMPLE 3

[sid 852]

Use a triple integral to find the volume of the solid region inside the sphere x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 6 and above the paraboloid z = x^2 + y^2.

Solution One-quarter of the required volume lies in the first octant. The two surfaces intersect on the vertical cylinder

    6 - x^2 - y^2 = z^2 = (x^2 + y^2)^2,

or, in terms of cylindrical coordinates, 6 - r^2 = r^4, that is,

    r^4 + r^2 - 6 = 0
    (r^2 + 3)(r^2 - 2) = 0.

The only relevant solution to this equation is r = sqrt(2). Thus, the required volume lies above the disk D of radius sqrt(2) centred at the origin in the xy-plane. The total volume V of the region is

    V = tripleintegral over R of dV = integral from 0 to 2pi of d(theta) integral from 0 to sqrt(2) of r dr integral from r^2 to sqrt(6 - r^2) of dz
      = 2pi · integral from 0 to sqrt(2) of r(sqrt(6 - r^2) - r^3) dr
      = 2pi · [-(1/3)(6 - r^2)^(3/2) - r^4/4] from 0 to sqrt(2)
      = 2pi · [-(1/3)(6 - 2)^(3/2) - 2/4 - (-(1/3) · 6^(3/2) + 0)]
      = 2pi · [(6·sqrt(6) - 8)/3 - 1]
      = (2/3)pi(6·sqrt(6) - 11) cubic units.


## Spherical Coordinates

Also introduced in Section 10.6 is the system of spherical coordinates related to Cartesian coordinates x, y, z, and cylindrical coordinates r, theta, z by the equations

    x = R sin(phi) cos(theta)
    y = R sin(phi) sin(theta)
    z = R cos(phi),

where

    R^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = r^2 + z^2,
    r = sqrt(x^2 + y^2) = R sin(phi),
    tan(phi) = r/z = sqrt(x^2 + y^2)/z    and    tan(theta) = y/x.

These relationships are illustrated in the standard figures for spherical coordinates. The coordinate surfaces for spherical coordinates are spheres of constant R (centred at the origin), circular cones with axes along the z-axis (for constant phi), and half-planes containing the z-axis (for constant theta).

The volume element in spherical coordinates is

    dV = R^2 sin(phi) dR d(phi) d(theta).

To see this, observe that the infinitesimal coordinate box bounded by the coordinate surfaces corresponding to values R, R + dR, phi, phi + d(phi), theta, and theta + d(theta) has dimensions dR, R d(phi), and R sin(phi) d(theta).

Alternatively, the Jacobian of the transformation can be calculated:

    partial(x,y,z)/partial(R,phi,theta) = | sin(phi) cos(theta)   R cos(phi) cos(theta)   -R sin(phi) sin(theta) |
                                          | sin(phi) sin(theta)   R cos(phi) sin(theta)    R sin(phi) cos(theta) |
                                          | cos(phi)             -R sin(phi)              0                      |
                                        = R^2 sin(phi).

[sid 853]

Spherical coordinates are suited to problems involving spherical symmetry and, in particular, to regions bounded by spheres centred at the origin, circular cones with axes along the z-axis, and vertical planes containing the z-axis.

## EXAMPLE 4

A solid half-ball H of radius a has density rho (mass per unit volume) depending on the distance R from the centre of the base disk. The density is given by rho = k(2a - R), where k is a constant. Find the mass of the half-ball.

Solution Choosing coordinates with origin at the centre of the base, so that the half-ball lies above the xy-plane, we calculate the mass m as follows:

    m = tripleintegral over H of k(2a - R) dV = tripleintegral over H of k(2a - R) R^2 sin(phi) dR d(phi) d(theta)
      = k integral from 0 to 2pi of d(theta) integral from 0 to pi/2 of sin(phi) d(phi) integral from 0 to a of (2a - R) R^2 dR
      = 2k·pi · 1 · [2a R^3/3 - R^4/4] from 0 to a
      = 2k·pi · (2a^4/3 - a^4/4)
      = ka^4/6 units.

Remark In the above example, both the integrand and the region of integration exhibited spherical symmetry, so the choice of spherical coordinates to carry out the integration was most appropriate. The mass could have been evaluated in cylindrical coordinates. The iteration in that system is

    m = integral from 0 to 2pi of d(theta) integral from 0 to a of r dr integral from 0 to sqrt(a^2 - r^2) of k(2a - sqrt(r^2 + z^2)) dz

and is difficult to evaluate. It is even more difficult in Cartesian coordinates:

    m = 4 integral from 0 to a of dx integral from 0 to sqrt(a^2 - x^2) of dy integral from 0 to sqrt(a^2 - x^2 - y^2) of k(2a - sqrt(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)) dz.

The choice of coordinate system can greatly affect the difficulty of computation of a multiple integral. Many problems will have elements of spherical and axial symmetry. In such cases it may not be clear whether it would be better to use spherical or cylindrical coordinates. In such doubtful cases the integrand is usually the best guide. Use cylindrical or spherical coordinates according to whether the integrand involves x^2 + y^2 or x^2 + y^2 + z^2.

## EXAMPLE 5

The moment of inertia about the z-axis of a solid of density rho occupying the region R is given by the integral

    I = tripleintegral over R of (x^2 + y^2) dV.

(See Section 14.7.) Calculate that moment of inertia for a solid of unit density occupying the region inside the sphere x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4a^2 and outside the cylinder x^2 + y^2 = a^2.

Solution See the figure for this example. In terms of spherical coordinates the required moment of inertia is

    I = 2 integral from 0 to 2pi of d(theta) integral from pi/6 to pi/2 of sin(phi) d(phi) integral from a/sin(phi) to 2a of R^2 sin^2(phi) R^2 dR.

In terms of cylindrical coordinates it is

    I = 2 integral from 0 to 2pi of d(theta) integral from a to 2a of r dr integral from 0 to sqrt(4a^2 - r^2) of r^2 dz.

The latter formula looks somewhat easier to evaluate. We continue with it. Evaluating the theta and z integrals, we get

    I = 4 integral from a to 2a of r^3 sqrt(4a^2 - r^2) dr.

Making the substitution u = 4a^2 - r^2, du = -2r dr, we obtain

    I = 2 integral from 0 to 3a^2 of (4a^2 - u) sqrt(u) du
      = 2[4a^2 · (2/3)u^(3/2) - (2/5)u^(5/2)] from 0 to 3a^2
      = (44/5)·sqrt(3)·a^5.
